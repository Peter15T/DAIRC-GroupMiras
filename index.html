Two hypotheses, two findings

What we set out to test.

H1 · Cultural — Perception ambiguity

Because synthetic influencers aim to mimic human-like authenticity cues, TikTok users often cannot reliably distinguish them from human influencers — instead judging authenticity on the content itself rather than the creator's identity.

Supported. Video 2 (AI in a fashion context) achieved only 23% correct identification — perception of authenticity is driven by production aesthetics rather than creator identity.
H2 · Political — Disclosure gap

Synthetic influencer content on TikTok is often not clearly disclosed, creating uncertainty about authenticity for users — and pointing to potential gaps in EU transparency requirements (DSA, AI Act, GDPR).

Supported. 87% of respondents endorse mandatory AI labelling, yet zero participants used a platform label as a detection cue. Labels not seen cannot function as governance instruments.

Motivation

Why this research now.

The rapid proliferation of AI-generated influencers on TikTok signals a structural transformation of the User-Generated Content ecosystem, wherein human authenticity is increasingly simulated through scalable, automated production. As synthetic personas replicate human-like communicative cues, they erode users' capacity to distinguish real from artificial identities. TikTok constitutes a critical case study given its algorithmic amplification mechanisms and accelerating integration of AI-driven content practices.

The OECD (2025) identifies AI-generated outputs as exponentially reshaping market dynamics across sectors, including digital marketing. Brands are correspondingly adopting synthetic influencers to optimize speed, cost-efficiency, and return on investment. While the EU regulatory framework — comprising the AI Act (Reg. 2024/1689), GDPR (Reg. 2016/679), and the Digital Services Act (Reg. 2022/2065) — establishes compliance boundaries, significant implementation gaps persist for platforms such as TikTok, particularly regarding transparency and disclosure obligations.

The literature gap

What was missing in the field.

Preliminary research reveals a scarcity of literature addressing the growing prevalence of AI-generated human-replicas within UGC. Existing studies mostly examine UGC's influence on consumer trust in general (Kim and Song, 2018), while others focus on narrow instances akin to Zidong Li and Youngsok Bang, largely analyzing the impact of online AI-summarized reviews (Li and Bang, 2026).

The OECD highlights the exponentiality of AI-generated outputs across various sectors (OECD, 2025). Simultaneously, more brands rely on "synthetic influencers" to produce content faster, cheaper, and increase ROI. Despite regulatory frameworks (AI Act, GDPR, DSA) establishing limits to AI, gaps remain in ensuring compliance of platforms like TikTok. It is therefore important to understand the extent to which TikTok users have a hard time distinguishing human from "synthetic" personas — and whether existing EU regulations sufficiently respond to the issues of transparency and authenticity that arise from this ambiguity.

The artifact

A pipeline, not just a paper.

The research outcome takes the form of an interactive website functioning simultaneously as an analytical artifact and a methodological pilot. The web medium mirrors the native digital environment of the object under study, reinforcing the epistemological alignment between form and content. The website enacts the very logic of platform-based content consumption (sequential, visual, comparative) while imposing analytical structure upon it.

Crucially, the artifact is conceived as a proof-of-concept pipeline. It demonstrates how synthetic influencer content can be systematically identified, categorized, and evaluated across dimensions of authenticity, disclosure, and audience perception. The project served as a pilot intended as a replicable model for more representative, large-scale research analyses — the kind that institutions such as regulatory bodies, academic research centers, or VLOPs subject to the DSA could operationalize at scale.

Methodology

A four-step triangulated pipeline.

We combined four complementary streams of evidence to test our hypotheses against both user perception and regulatory text — ensuring triangulation across data sources while adhering to ethical standards (consent, anonymization, platform compliance).

Step I · Content scraping

180 → 35 videos

180 TikTok videos initially scraped via Apify (GDPR-compliant). Manually curated content from TikTok Ads Library (Jan–Mar 2026), filtered by category — beauty, lifestyle, fashion. Final dataset: 35 videos.

Step II · Online survey

39 respondents

4 videos (3 synthetic, 1 human) shown via Google Form. ~60% aged 18–24. 53.8% female. Quantitative analysis in Excel + Python (pandas), particularly for multi-select responses.

Step III · Interviews

3 creators · 3 languages

Semi-structured interviews (~30 min) in French, English, and Georgian — recruited via personal networks. Recorded with consent, anonymized (Influencer1, 2, 3), transcribed via Goodtape.

Step IV · Desk research

3 EU frameworks

Qualitative analysis of GDPR, DSA, EU AI Act and secondary literature (TikTok privacy policy, AI Incident Database, Data Protection Commission rulings).

Limitations

Difficulties encountered, and deviations.

While we largely followed the triangulated mixed-method approach planned at the start of the semester, several difficulties were encountered — particularly regarding the survey, leading to deviations from the initial research design.

  • TikTok Research API restriction. The initial plan to collect videos exclusively from the TikTok Ads Library proved too restrictive, as an insufficient number of realistic AI-generated videos could be identified. We expanded scope to the entire TikTok platform — increasing feasibility but reducing standardization.
  • Selecting genuinely ambiguous videos was difficult. Many videos that appeared clearly AI-generated to us were also easily identifiable by participants, limiting our ability to test perceptual ambiguity. Many influencers also explicitly mention "AI" in captions while demonstrating its use.
  • AI-personas vs AI-assisted editing. Distinguishing how AI was used posed a methodological challenge, particularly between fully AI-generated personas and AI-assisted editing processes — creating ambiguity in operationalizing key variables.
  • Source-disclosure bias. For legal and ethical reasons, video sources had to be provided within the survey. Respondents could therefore access the original content and potentially identify whether it was AI-generated prior to answering, introducing a possible bias.
  • Convenience sampling. Due to time constraints, the survey was distributed within immediate networks and Sciences Po students. Age was used as a proxy for socio-professional category (students, workers, retirees). The sample skews young: 62% aged 18–24, with only 7 respondents aged 30+.
  • No clean Kaggle dataset. No systematic dataset on AI-generated human influencers exists. Closest available: AI Diverse Portraits and Generative AI: Creating Machines More Human-Like.
  • Interviewee withdrawal. One participant withdrew at the last minute. We managed to interview three influencers, as initially planned.
  • Binary question design primes detection behaviour and likely inflates correct identification rates relative to organic conditions.
  • Pre-enforcement window. EU AI Act Article 50 — the most directly applicable transparency obligation — does not enter into application until August 2026, meaning the regulatory landscape we examine remains voluntary.
Two narratives

The story we tell.

The interactive narrative communicates two primary insights:

i · The Authenticity Paradox

Side-by-side comparisons of AI avatars and human influencers illustrate how "Human-like Virtual Influencers" (HVIs) build cognitive trust through anthropomorphic cues — while simultaneously risking an "uncanny valley" effect that damages brand reputation.

ii · Regulatory Friction & Governance

The project analyses how AI use on TikTok aligns with EU frameworks (GDPR, DSA, AI Act), highlighting the tension between the "data hunger" required to train AI models and European standards for data minimization, purpose limitation, and transparency.

What the four tests teach us

Detection is highly context-dependent. Close-up content (Videos 1 & 3) gives away its synthetic nature through micro-expressions and skin texture — the uncanny valley still bites. But cinematic, high-production content (Video 2) breaks the system: 55% of respondents wrongly classified an AI fashion campaign as real human models.

When asked why they distrusted the AI Video 1, respondents consistently invoked the absence of embodied experience rather than synthetic appearance: "if it's AI it means the influencer never tested it"; "it is not implemented on a real face"; "that's not a genuine opinion." Distrust of AI content is therefore not primarily perceptual but epistemic — anchored in the belief that recommendation requires lived experience.

Synthesis

Why detection and disclosure are misaligned.

Our project helped us understand how the mechanism governing the detection of synthetic influencers is misaligned with the mechanism governing disclosure. Respondents relied on embodied visual cues (facial texture, eye contact, gut feeling) that generation quality will soon render unreliable — while ignoring the one instrument designed to compensate: the platform AI label, cited by zero respondents as a detection cue, despite 87% endorsing mandatory labelling.

Labels that are not seen cannot function as governance instruments.

The desk research refines this further: GDPR governs data flows, the AI Act governs technical outputs, the DSA governs platform conduct — but none governs the synthetic identity itself. All three frameworks were designed before synthetic influencers existed as a phenomenon. Article 50 of the AI Act, the most directly applicable provision, does not enter into force until August 2026. The landscape studied here operates entirely in a pre-enforcement window where TikTok's voluntary labelling policy is the only operative safeguard — and one that, by our evidence, is not reaching users.

For policymakers, the findings suggest disclosure obligations alone are insufficient. Effective regulation would need to address label placement, format, and salience within platform interfaces — questions of design rather than legislation, and ones that require coordination between regulators and platforms that the current DSA architecture does not mandate in sufficient detail.

Page 03 · Desk review

Three EU laws, one missing piece.

A complete review of the three EU frameworks most relevant to synthetic influencers — DSA, AI Act, and GDPR. We assess each framework's coverage, definitions, articles, and enforcement gaps as they apply to TikTok and synthetic personas specifically.

Overall verdict

The three regulatory frameworks examined reveal a cumulative governance gap when applied to the specific phenomenon of synthetic influencers on TikTok.

Existing EU regulation covers the infrastructure of AI content (GDPR), its technical outputs (AI Act), and its distribution platforms (DSA) — but none specifically governs the synthetic identity itself, leaving a structural gap at the precise point where authenticity perception is most at stake.

✓ Regulation (EU) 2022/2065

Digital Services Act — most operationally relevant, still insufficient.

The DSA is the most operationally proximate framework, as it formally designates TikTok as a VLOP (Very Large Online Platform) and imposes binding obligations around algorithmic transparency, systemic risk assessment, and advertising disclosure.

However, a Ctrl+F search of the regulation returns zero results for "synthetic," "AI-generated," "artificial persona," or "influencer." The DSA's obligations are structured around platform conduct rather than the ontological status of content producers. As a result, a fully AI-generated influencer account operating on TikTok falls outside any explicitly defined regulatory category within the DSA.

Three structural gaps

  1. Content-type gap. The DSA regulates platform behaviour, not content identity. No provision specifically targets AI-generated personas or synthetic influencers as a category.
  2. Definition gap. Article 3(r) defines an "advertisement" as content presented "against remuneration." Researchers have called for this to be expanded to cover content serving a commercial purpose regardless of payment. An unpaid synthetic influencer organically promoting a brand may fall entirely outside DSA advertising obligations.
  3. Enforcement gap. On 5 December 2025, the Commission imposed a €120 million fine on X — the first DSA non-compliance decision. Crucially, TikTok was not sanctioned: the Commission accepted voluntary commitments on advertising transparency rather than issuing a fine.

Article-by-article assessment

Art. 33
VLOP designation
TikTok formally designated VLOP since April 2023 — directly subject to the DSA's most stringent obligations.
Art. 27
Recommender transparency
Requires disclosure of recommender parameters in plain language. Does not restrict AI-generated content promotion via the For You Page.
Art. 34
Risk assessment
Yearly assessment of systemic risks. TikTok's reports have not addressed AI-generated personas as a distinct risk category.
Art. 35
Risk mitigation
Tailored mitigation measures required. No specific measure targets synthetic influencer content. The Commission's preliminary February 2026 finding flagged TikTok for breaching this article.
Art. 26 + 3(r)
Advertising transparency
All ads must be labelled. Synthetic influencer promotion may not constitute "advertising" if no direct payment exists.
Art. 28(2)
Protection of minors
Bans profiling-based targeting of minors. No specific provision on AI-generated personas targeting minors.
Art. 38
Recommender alternatives
Non-profiling feed required. Synthetic content circulates equally — the issue is identity, not personalisation logic.
Art. 40
Researcher data access
TikTok found non-compliant by the Commission for overly restrictive data-sharing — limiting research like ours.

Sources: EUR-Lex 32022R2065 · EUCRIM "EU fines X €120m" (Dec 2025) · MediaLaws (TikTok DSA breaches) · Verfassungsblog (TikTok-DSA preliminary finding) · Commission press release on TikTok addictive design (Feb 2026).

✓ Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

EU AI Act — direct, but not yet enforceable.

The AI Act represents the most direct regulatory response to AI-generated content to date. Its disclosure obligations (Art. 50) and deepfake definition (Art. 3(60)) are conceptually relevant — yet two structural weaknesses limit their applicability to synthetic influencers specifically.

Three structural gaps

  1. Scope gap. The deepfake definition (Art. 3(60)) targets impersonation of real persons. Fully fictional synthetic influencers — a growing share of AI-generated TikTok content — may not qualify as deepfakes and thus evade the regulation's core transparency obligations.
  2. Enforcement gap. Article 50 does not apply until August 2026. The entire landscape of synthetic influencer content examined in this research operates in a pre-enforcement vacuum, where compliance remains voluntary and platform-driven rather than legally mandated.
  3. Platform gap. The Act does not establish platform-specific obligations for VLOPs like TikTok regarding AI-generated content. The DSA-AI Act interface (Recital 136) is acknowledged but underdeveloped.

Verdict table

Art. 50(2)
Synthetic content disclosure
Machine-readable marking required for AI-generated audio/image/video/text. Obligation falls on providers (tool developers), not creators posting on TikTok.
Art. 50(4)
Deepfake labelling
Most directly applicable to synthetic influencers. Enforcement begins August 2026 — non-binding during this study.
Art. 3(60)
Deepfake definition
Threshold is perception-based. Gap: purely invented synthetic influencers who do not impersonate a real person may fall outside this definition.
Art. 50(5)
First-exposure disclosure
Disclosure must occur "at the latest at the time of the first interaction." On TikTok's autoplay feed, practically unenforceable without platform-level integration.
Art. 99
Fines
Up to €15M or 3% of global turnover. With enforcement deferred to 2026 and no platform-specific mechanism, deterrence remains theoretical.
Art. 50(4) exception
Artistic / satirical loophole
"Evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional" content benefits from reduced disclosure — could be exploited by synthetic accounts framing themselves as fictional entertainment.

The pre-enforcement window

Recital 133 explicitly identifies the risks of synthetic content generation — misinformation at scale, fraud, impersonation, consumer deception — confirming legislative intent. The Draft Code of Practice envisages a common taxonomy distinguishing fully AI-generated from AI-assisted content, mirroring our three analytical categories. Until August 2026, however, TikTok's internal labelling policy operates as de facto soft governance in the absence of binding legal obligation.

Sources: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, OJ L, 12.7.2024 · artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50 · Barata Mir, J. (2025), Transparency Obligations for All AI Systems: Article 50 of the AI Act, CCIA.

✓ Regulation (EU) 2016/679

GDPR — too broad to address synthetic identity.

The GDPR, as a technology-neutral instrument designed in 2016, provides foundational data-protection principles but was never conceived to address AI-generated identities, platform-level content dynamics, or the authenticity ambiguities that synthetic personas introduce.

The word "social media" does not appear once in the regulation. Its provisions operate at the level of personal data processing rather than content production or identity simulation.

Articles examined

Recital 6 + 30
Online identifiers
Acknowledges that online identifiers (cookies, IPs) can profile natural persons — but predates large-scale synthetic content generation by nearly a decade.
Art. 4(1)
Personal data definition
"Any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person." Synthetic personas are by definition not natural persons — so do not trigger GDPR protection of their own "data."
Art. 5(e)
Storage limitation
Personal data may not be kept longer than necessary. Tension with the "data hunger" required to train AI models that simulate human authenticity.
Art. 6
Lawfulness of processing
Six legal bases for data processing. None squarely captures the use of personal data to train synthetic personas.
Art. 12
Transparent information
Information must be "concise, transparent, intelligible." Concept transferable but not operationalised for synthetic content.
Art. 17
Right to erasure
"Right to be forgotten" — but how do you "erase" personal data already absorbed into a generative model's weights?

The Irish DPC fine — a recent stress test

In April 2025, the Irish Data Protection Commission fined TikTok €530 million for non-compliant transfers of EEA user data to China via remote access (breaching Art. 46 and transparency requirements). TikTok appealed — the High Court suspended the order pending appeal. The case shows the GDPR's reach over data flows, but does not address the synthetic identity question central to our research.

Sources: Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR original text) · TikTok Privacy Policy · Irish DPC ruling, April 2025 (€530M fine) · AI Incident Database (301 TikTok-related reports as of project date).

Synthesis

Three frameworks, one missing category.

Read together, the GDPR addresses data inputs, the AI Act addresses technical outputs, and the DSA addresses platform distribution. The synthetic influencer sits at the centre of these three — yet is not the explicit subject of any one of them. The identity itself, the persona that earns trust and shapes consumer behaviour, falls into a regulatory void.

Existing EU regulation covers the infrastructure of AI content, its technical outputs, and its distribution platforms — but none specifically governs the synthetic identity itself.

Authenticity as theatre

Influencer1's defining contribution is to deconstruct the very notion of authenticity. For her, no social-media content is authentic — every creator, even the most "real," is performing a strategic version of relatability. This converges directly with our theoretical framing: if even human creators "theatricalise" authenticity, the human/AI distinction becomes even more porous.

"Il n'y a aucun contenu authentique. Même quand on a l'air authentique, on théâtralise l'authenticité."

Key positions

  • Doesn't use AI for scripting — only CapCut for editing/captions.
  • Believes labelling on TikTok is "not at all clear."
  • Detection cues she names are linguistic: ternary rhythm, "intelligence stratégique," repetitive contrastive structures.
  • Generation gap: 40+ users struggle to detect; younger users do better.
  • Her view: the problem is not lack of rules, but rules that aren't enforced.

Cross-interview synthesis

Three definitions of authenticity emerge. Influencer1 sees it as performance — strategic theatricalisation of relatability. Influencer2 sees it as coherence with a real "core" self. Influencer3 sees it as evolving honesty — a dynamic relationship between creator and audience that AI cannot replicate. The tension between these views is itself a finding: if creators themselves disagree on what authenticity means, the regulatory ambition to label "AI-generated content" rests on shaky conceptual ground.

Detection cues differ across creators. Influencer1 cites linguistic tells — sentence rhythm, vocabulary. Influencer2 cites visual tells — eyes, micro-expressions. Influencer3 cites movement and "overall perfection." Together they cover what our survey respondents flagged: facial expressions (95 citations) and skin texture (64) on the visual side; voice patterns (75% in Test 4) on the linguistic side.

AI use is peripheral, not central. All three creators use AI for editing, captions, or supporting tasks — but only Influencer3 admits to using it for scripts or captions, and even she keeps it minimal. None delegates the script entirely. This matches Q5 of our survey: 53.8% of users use generative AI "often," but our study shows the script remains the last bastion of human authorship.

Disclosure: doubts and contradictions. Influencer1 sees no labels and judges TikTok's system "not at all clear." Influencer2 knows and praises the system but acknowledges its practical limits — slip-ups, low reading comprehension. Influencer3 reports that TikTok's labels are inconsistent and sometimes wrongly applied to real content. All three end up in the same place: the rules exist (or are coming), but enforcement and user attention are the real problem — exactly aligned with the H2 finding from the survey.
